Construction and rehab 7 minute read

How Construction Draws Actually Work, and Why Turnaround Time Decides Whether Your Project Stays on Schedule

A construction or rehab loan does not hand you the renovation budget at closing. It holds that money back and releases it in pieces as the work gets done. Those pieces are called draws, and understanding how they move is the difference between a project that finishes on time and one that drifts for months.

Why lenders hold the money back

The reason is straightforward. A lender's security is the property, and a half-finished renovation is worth less than either the original house or the completed one. Releasing the full budget up front would mean the loan is least secured at exactly the moment the borrower has the most cash in hand. Draws keep the money and the work moving in step with each other.

That logic protects you too, more than most borrowers appreciate. A draw structure is the reason a contractor cannot collect the entire budget in week one and then get slow.

How a draw request actually moves

You complete a phase of work. You submit a draw request through the lender's online platform, identifying what was finished and what is being requested. The lender reviews the request, typically with some form of verification that the work described was actually done. Once approved, funds are released.

With us, that release typically happens within 24 hours of approval, and draws are unlimited across the life of the project. There is no cap on how many you can take and no penalty for needing a fourth or a fifth.

Why the turnaround number matters more than it looks

Here is the part that does not show up on a rate comparison. Every day your project runs, you are paying for it: interest, insurance, taxes, utilities, and the opportunity cost of capital that is tied up rather than working on the next deal. Those are your carrying costs, and they accrue whether or not anyone is swinging a hammer.

Now consider what happens when a draw takes two weeks instead of one day. Your crew finishes a phase and waits to get paid. Good trades do not wait. They take the next job, and when your money finally arrives they are three weeks into someone else's project. You have not lost two weeks. You have lost two weeks plus however long it takes to get back in their schedule, and you are paying carrying costs for the whole gap.

Run that twice on a single project and a slow draw process has cost you more than any realistic difference in rate would have saved. This is why experienced flippers ask about draw turnaround before they ask about points, and why newer investors almost never think to ask at all.

What to ask any lender before you sign

  • How many draws am I allowed, and is there a fee per draw?
  • How do I submit a request, and what evidence do you need with it?
  • How is the work verified, and does that require someone to visit the site?
  • Once a draw is approved, how long until the funds actually land?
  • Is there a minimum draw amount that would force me to batch requests?

Ask for the answers in specifics rather than in adjectives. "Fast" is not an answer. A number is.

How to keep your own draws moving

Most delays are not the lender's doing. Submit requests for work that is genuinely complete rather than nearly complete. Keep your documentation tidy as you go instead of assembling it under pressure. Sequence your phases so a single slow inspection does not stall three trades at once. And talk to your lender before a phase runs long, not after.

Underwriting 6 minute read

What a Term Sheet Should Mean, and Why Some Lenders Issue Them Casually

A term sheet is a lender telling you the shape of the deal they are prepared to do: the structure, the broad terms, and the conditions attached. What it is supposed to signal is intent. What it too often signals is nothing at all.

The failure everyone in this business has seen

An investor gets a term sheet. They rely on it. They waive contingencies, put earnest money at risk, and tell the seller they are good. Then, somewhere in the last two weeks, the lender surfaces a problem that was visible from the beginning, and the deal dies. The investor loses the earnest money, the contract, and the relationship with a seller who will not bring them the next one.

The frustrating part is that in most of these cases nothing genuinely new was discovered. The issue was always in the file. It was simply never looked at until the end, because issuing the term sheet cost the lender nothing.

Why casual term sheets exist

Because they work as a sales tool. A term sheet takes a borrower off the market and stops them shopping. If a lender issues twenty and funds twelve, they have still won twelve deals, and the eight investors left holding a dead contract are a cost that lands entirely on the investor.

It also happens without bad intent. A lender who does not control their own capital may issue terms in good faith and then discover their source will not take the file. The result for you is the same either way.

What should already have happened before you get one

A term sheet worth relying on comes after somebody has looked at the things most likely to break the deal. That means a real view of the property and the plan, a preliminary look at title, an understanding of your entity structure and who is guaranteeing, a sanity check on your liquidity and experience against the program, and confirmation that the capital source is actually available for this type of file.

None of that requires a full underwrite. It requires somebody experienced spending an hour on the hard parts instead of the easy ones.

Questions to ask when a term sheet lands

  • What have you already reviewed to get to these terms?
  • What conditions remain, and which of them worry you most?
  • Is the capital for this loan already identified, or still being sourced?
  • What would cause you to withdraw these terms?
  • Has anything about my file given you pause that we have not discussed?

That last question is the useful one. A lender who has genuinely looked will have an answer. A lender who has not will give you a reassurance.

The honest limit

No lender can promise a loan will close. All loans remain subject to underwriting, property review, and final approval, and anyone who tells you otherwise is either misinformed or misleading you. Valuations come in low. Title surfaces something nobody knew about. Conditions change.

What a lender can control is whether they front-load the hard questions or save them for the end. We do not issue term sheets casually, and when we commit to a deal, we are committed to getting it to the closing table. You should not be surprised in the final week by something we could have raised in the first conversation.

Strategy 6 minute read

Fix and Flip Financing or a DSCR Loan for Your First Rental?

These two structures get compared constantly, usually by investors trying to work out which one is cheaper. That is the wrong question. They solve different problems, and the right one depends entirely on what you intend to do with the property.

The short version

Fix and flip financing is short term money for buying and renovating a property. It is built around an exit that happens fairly quickly, usually a sale. A DSCR loan is long term financing for a rental, qualified on whether the property's income covers its debt rather than on your personal income documentation. It is built around holding.

If you are buying a distressed property to renovate and sell, you want the first one. If you are buying a property that is already rentable and you intend to keep it, you want the second.

The case that actually confuses people

The genuinely ambiguous scenario is a property that needs work and that you intend to keep as a rental. Both structures are technically available and investors routinely pick wrong.

A DSCR loan is qualified on the rent the property produces. A house that cannot be occupied does not produce rent, which is why a heavy renovation project usually cannot be financed this way from the start. So the common path is two stages: short term financing to buy and renovate, then a refinance into long term financing once the property is stabilized and leased.

That path works well and it is used constantly. It is also where first-time investors get hurt, because it depends on a refinance that has not happened yet. If the property does not appraise where you expected, or the rent does not land where you projected, or your circumstances change, you are holding short term debt with no exit. Before you start a two-stage plan, you should know roughly what the refinance is going to look like.

What changes about the math

Short term financing is more expensive per month and less expensive overall, because you are only in it briefly. Long term financing is cheaper per month and far more expensive across its life, which is the correct trade when you are holding the asset for years. Comparing their rates head to head tells you almost nothing, because you are comparing the cost of six months against the cost of thirty years.

What matters is the total cost of getting to your actual endpoint. For a flip, that is purchase plus renovation plus carrying costs plus selling costs, against the resale figure. For a rental, it is whether the property still produces income you are satisfied with after the permanent debt is in place.

What changes about the underwriting

Fix and flip underwriting focuses on the project: the scope of work, the budget, who is doing it, the timeline, and whether the resale figure is supported by what has genuinely sold nearby. DSCR underwriting focuses on the property as an income producer: what it rents for, what the debt costs, and whether the first comfortably covers the second.

The practical implication is that a strong flip file and a strong DSCR file look different. Bringing contractor bids to a DSCR conversation, or a rent projection to a flip conversation, means you have prepared the wrong thing.

How to decide

Answer one question first: what does this property look like in eighteen months? If you have sold it, you want short term financing. If you still own it and a tenant is paying you, you want to end up in a DSCR loan, and the only remaining question is whether you can get there directly or need to renovate first.

Answer that honestly, before you get attached to a rate. If you are not sure, that is a good conversation to have with a lender rather than a good reason to guess. Ask us about your situation.

Underwriting 7 minute read

What a Lender Is Actually Looking At When They Underwrite Your Deal

Underwriting feels opaque from the borrower's side, which is why it generates so much anxiety. It is not actually mysterious. A lender is trying to answer one question, which is whether this loan gets repaid, and they answer it by examining four things.

1. The property

This is the security, so it gets the most scrutiny. What is the property worth today, what will it be worth after the planned work, what condition is it in, and what does the location support? For a rental, what does it realistically rent for.

This is also where deals most often move rather than die. A valuation coming in below expectation does not necessarily end a transaction. It changes the numbers, and the question becomes whether the deal still works at the real figure.

2. The plan

What are you going to do with this property, how long will it take, and what will it cost? On a renovation, that means a real scope of work with real pricing, not a round number. On a construction project, a budget and a draw schedule. On a value-add multifamily deal, what the units rent for now, what they should rent for, and what it costs to close that gap.

The tell that an underwriter is looking for is specificity. A borrower who can describe the plan in detail has usually thought it through. A borrower whose numbers are all round has usually not.

3. The exit

This is the one borrowers underweight most, and it is arguably the most important. How does the loan get repaid? Sale, refinance, or something else, and what has to be true for that to happen on schedule.

A vague exit is the single most common reason a file that looks strong on paper gets declined. "I will probably refinance" is not an exit. "I will refinance into a DSCR loan once it is leased, and here is roughly what that looks like at today's numbers" is an exit.

4. The borrower

Not your life story, and for a business purpose loan not the exhaustive income documentation a consumer mortgage requires. Mainly: have you done this before, do you have reserves to handle things going wrong, is your credit profile consistent with the program, and what entity is borrowing.

Experience matters most on complicated projects and least on simple ones. That is why a first-time investor can absolutely get financed on a straightforward deal and would struggle to get financed on a ground-up build.

What actually stalls files late

In practice, the late problems are rarely about the four items above. They are about details nobody looked at early: a title issue, an entity that was never properly formed, a contractor who turns out to be unlicensed for the work, insurance that does not meet requirements, or liquidity that was described optimistically and does not survive verification.

Every one of those is discoverable in the first week. The reason they surface in the last week is that nobody went looking sooner.

How to make your file easy to underwrite

  • Bring the actual numbers, including the ones that make your deal look harder
  • Have your entity documents in order before you need them
  • Be able to describe your exit in one specific sentence
  • Know your real liquidity after the down payment, not before it
  • Raise the awkward detail yourself rather than hoping it goes unnoticed

That last one is worth more than people expect. Nothing you disclose up front will hurt you as much as the same fact discovered in week six. A lender who knows about a problem early can usually structure around it. A lender who finds it late has run out of room.
